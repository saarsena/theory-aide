## Move the home

Start on **Minor, home A** and press play: dark, unresolved, the sound of a
thousand sad songs. Now switch the home to **Major, home C**. Listen closely,
because this is the trick: the notes did not change. Both scales are the white
keys. Only the note you rest on moved.

That is the entire idea, and it is bigger than minor. If moving home from C to
A gives you a whole new mood out of the same notes, then there must be five
other homes in there too, five other moods hiding in one set of seven notes.
There are, and they are the modes. Minor is simply the most famous of them,
the one that earned its own name.

## The same notes, a new center

C major and A minor are built from identical pitches, so they are called
**[[relative key|relative keys]]**: same notes, different home. Every major
scale has a relative minor sitting a minor third below its home (the [[triads|minor third]] from the triads article, three semitones down), and every minor
scale has a relative major a minor third above. This is why the
[[the-circle-of-fifths|circle of fifths]] pairs them: relatives share a key
signature because they share every note.

The version you just heard, the pure "same notes, new home" scale, is called
**natural minor**. It is the honest minor, the one the modes are made of. But
it has a problem, and fixing that problem is where the other two minors come
from.

## Why minor sounds minor

Before the fix, the mood. What makes minor *sound* minor is one note: the
third. Home to third in major is four semitones, the bright major third; in
minor it is three, the [[triads|minor third]]. Everything else can wait, that
one lowered note is most of the darkness. If you have read the triads article
this is the same major-versus-minor switch you heard there, now stretched
across a whole scale instead of a single chord. Minor is a scale built on a
minor third the way major is built on a major one.

## Three minors, and the pull toward home

Natural minor's problem is the top. In major, the seventh note sits one
semitone below home and leans hard upward into it; that magnetic note is the
**[[leading tone]]**, and it is what makes a major cadence feel like an
arrival. Natural minor has no leading tone. Its seventh is a whole step below
home (G up to A), a gentle lean, not a pull, so endings feel soft and open.

Composers wanted the pull back. So:

- **Harmonic minor** raises the seventh a semitone (G becomes G#), rebuilding
  the leading tone. Now minor can slam home as decisively as major. The cost is
  a wide, exotic gap that opens between the sixth and the raised seventh, the
  sound your ear files under "Spanish" or "Middle Eastern."
- **Melodic minor** smooths that gap by *also* raising the sixth on the way up
  (F and G both lift to F# and G#), so an ascending line glides. Classically
  the raises are undone coming back down; in jazz the raised version is kept
  and becomes a scale all its own.

Switch between the three in the demo and listen only to the top of each climb.
The story of the three minors is really the story of one missing magnet and two
different ways of putting it back.

## In your music

Reach for **natural minor** when you want the open, modal, unresolved feeling
of most pop, rock, and electronic music, it does not beg to resolve, so it
loops forever without tiring. Reach for **harmonic minor** at a cadence when
you want minor to land with real finality, or when you want that dramatic
raised-seventh color on purpose. **Melodic minor** is a melodist's and a jazz
player's tool, for lines that need to climb smoothly.

And hold onto the keyhole, because the next article walks straight through it.
If A is one home inside the white keys and it gives you minor, then D is another
home, and E another, and each has its own name and its own flavor built from
those same seven notes. Those are [[modes|the modes]], and you already
understand exactly how they work.
