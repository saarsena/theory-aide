## One chord from the other world

One note moved. The F chord's A bent down to Ab, IV became iv, and the
loop aches on its way home. The Beatles leaned on that chord in "In My
Life," Radiohead built the hook of "Creep" on it, and once you can name
it you will start hearing it everywhere a major song needs one bar of
grief. Nothing about the key changed. C is still home; the chord is
simply not from here.

## Parallel, not relative

[[minor-scales|Minor scales]] taught you one mirror: C major and A minor
are [[relative key|relative keys]], the same notes with home moved. This
page runs on the other mirror. C major and C minor are [[parallel
key|parallel keys]]: the same home with the notes swapped, three of them
bent flat. Relatives share everything except their center; parallels
share *only* their center, which is exactly why borrowing works.
Because home never moves, your ear keeps its bearings, and a chord
imported from the parallel key reads as color, not as a key change. The
gravity stays; the light changes.

## The usual suspects

Three loans cover most of what popular music borrows from minor, and the
demo has one preset for each:

- **iv**, the minor four: heartbreak. The brightest spot in the major
  key, momentarily overcast. Strongest right before a return home, where
  its Ab falls one semitone to G inside the I chord.
- **bVII**, the flat seven: rock's house chord. You met its source note
  in [[modes|Modes]], Mixolydian's flat 7th; bVII is that color hardened
  into a whole chord. It brightens without resolving, which is why loops
  built on it can run all night.
- **bVI**, the flat six: the epic lift. Usually traveling with bVII in
  the walk-up bVI, bVII, I, the cadence of anthems, trailers, and final
  choruses since the sixties.

Each one is a mode's characteristic color, borrowed for a single bar
while home holds. Systematize the habit, raid all seven parallel modes
instead of just minor, and you have [[modal interchange]]: a Ring 3
pantry this page only cracks the door on.

## In your music

The extension knows these chords by name. Run **Explain Harmony** on a
clip in a major key and the borrowed imports wear an amber **borrowed**
badge: the loops in this demo come back labeled I, IV, iv, I and I,
bVII, IV, I, with iv, bVII, and bVI flagged, and the Harmonic Timeline
carries the same badges across a whole arrangement. Two experiments
worth stealing tonight: find the IV chord in the bar before your chorus
resolves and bend its third down a semitone, instant minor iv, maximum
effect per note moved; and take any loop that ends on V and try bVII
there instead, trading the pull for that open, running-downhill
brightness. If the loan feels better than the original, congratulations:
you have started cooking from the pantry, and the climb's summit, which
chords welcome which scales over them, is where that road leads next.
