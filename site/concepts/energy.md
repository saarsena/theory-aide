## The budget

Here is the steelman of "progressions are a trap." A dance track's
biggest gesture is the [[build]] and the [[drop]], and the drop is a
[[cadences|cadence]] transposed up the ladder: a section-level arrival,
prepared by a section-level dominant. Solberg's research on build-ups
found the drop's power comes precisely from staged removal and return
of layers, bass, and drive: arrangement tension, spent at the moment of
impact. Now suppose your chords are *also* running a journey with its
own full stops. Two tension budgets are now fighting over one listener,
and the smaller one loses. Worse, the DJ context punishes harmonic
closure: a mix needs sections that stay open, and a loop that
[[progressions|banks its pull]] mixes; a phrase that keeps filing full
stops argues with the blend. One chord, six minutes, packed floor is
not a failure of imagination. It is a budget allocated entirely to the
rungs where that room actually listens.

And yet "trap" is a rulebook word, and the counterexamples are not
edge cases. Deep house lives on seventh-chord loops that are absolutely
progressions. Trance runs the same vi-IV-I-V journeys as any power
ballad. French house is sampled disco *changes*. The honest sentence
is smaller and more useful: **a progression is not a trap, it is a
purchase, and you should know what else the money was for.**

## The third way: imply the journey

There is a move that gets you both, and it comes straight out of
[[modes|Modes]]: the bass owns the home, but the melody owns the
*color*. Park the bass forever, dance music's requirement satisfied,
and shift the mode of what plays above it, and a progression happens
anyway, in the listener's ear, without one bass note moving:

This is not a metaphor, and the extension can testify. Feed the
shifting preset's notes to the Harmonic Timeline and it reports chords
with real names, Dm, Dm7, Gadd9/D, Bbmaj7/D, and home again, gives the
Bb chord a roman numeral (VImaj7), flags B as a **dorian** color note
("natural 6th color against a minor key center," in its own words),
and rates the harmonic tension rising from 0 to 33 and settling back
to 0. An [[implied harmony|implied progression]], measured. The bass
paid for none of it; the [[chord-scale-thinking|turned notes]] paid
for all of it. If everything you write is modal, this is not a
consolation prize for skipping progressions. It is how progressions
sound when the bass has better things to do.

## Ask the concert hall

If implied progression over a static floor sounds like a producer's
workaround, the concert hall would like a word, because an entire
tradition made it the whole art form. Terry Riley's *In C* is
loop-based music from 1964: fifty-three loops over a pulsing C,
harmonic weather rolling through as the loops stack and shed, no DAW
required. Steve Reich's *Music for 18 Musicians* breathes through
eleven chords in an hour, tension carried almost entirely by pulse,
density, and register, the arrangement journey played by human lungs;
his essay "Music as a Gradual Process" is the aesthetic argument in
four pages. This tradition, [[minimalism]], is dance music's older
cousin, and Butler's study of EDM points at the family resemblance
from the other side.

So: maybe progressions *are* a trap in a deep-house ambient track.
But ask the Bang on a Can All-Stars about implied progressions: the
ensemble performs Brian Eno's *Music for Airports*, ambient's founding
record, live, as concert repertoire, sustaining an hour of music whose
harmony is almost all implication and decay. The same evening's
program might hold Julia Wolfe or Michael Gordon, postminimalism built
on grooves any techno producer would recognize. The people who took
static harmony most seriously did not treat progression as a trap.
They treated it as a *dial*, and spent careers exploring the quiet end
of it.

## In your music

The extension gives this argument meters. The Composition Dimensions
panel scores vertical (harmony) against macro (arrangement energy), so
you can see your own budget allocation; the Harmonic Timeline's
tension track shows where the harmonic spending happens; and its modal
color flags catch implied shifts the way they caught the dorian B
above. Three experiments, one per section of this page: take a loop
whose chords move and freeze them, then rebuild the same lift with
density and register alone, one layer per bar; take a static loop and
give it four bars of real changes, and notice what the drop feels like
afterward, and whether the two journeys fought; and park a drone under
your next idea, then turn one note of the melody per bar, dorian's
sixth, aeolian's flat six, and let the timeline tell you what
progression you implied. None of these is the right answer. The point
is that it was always a budget, never a rule, and now you can read
the ledger.
