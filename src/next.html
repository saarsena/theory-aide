What Do I Do Next?

Turning one good progression, melody, or bass line into the next part of a song

When you generate something you like, do not immediately ask for a totally new idea. First identify what is working, then create a related contrast. Songs usually move forward by changing one or two musical dimensions while keeping the rest familiar.

Guided Next Move

Freeze The Idea

Loop the part and write down the facts. You are trying to learn what the idea is, not whether it is theoretically fancy.

Keep

  • Key or tonal center
  • Main chord loop
  • One memorable rhythm
  • One signature interval, hook, or bass movement

Label

  • Does it feel like home, lift, tension, or release?
  • Is the energy low, medium, or high?
  • Is the section sparse, full, dark, bright, stable, or unstable?

Decide The Job Of The Next Section

I find it useful to pick a lane. If there is oncoming traffic, as in it sounds awful and nothing works, pick another. You have choices.

You can:

Continue the groove Open up Build tension Resolve Contrast Prepare the drop or chorus

Or metaphorically break everything, pick up the pieces, and move on with the verse.

Change One Big Thing

If you change everything, it sounds like a new song. If you change nothing, it loops forever. Change one big thing and one small thing.

Harmony: keep the bass rhythm, but move to a related chord start such as vi, IV, ii, or V.
Melody: keep the rhythm, but move the notes higher, lower, or answer the phrase in shorter fragments.
Bass: keep the chords, but simplify the bass for a verse or make it more active for a build.
Texture: keep the notes, but change instrumentation, octave, density, or drum activity.

Common Next Moves

From a loop to a verse

Remove notes. Use fewer drums. Let the bass and vocal space define the section. If the generated progression is busy, use only its first two chords or a pad version.

From a loop to a chorus

Raise the melody range. Add chord tones on top. Make the bass land harder on roots. Keep the same harmonic DNA, but make the rhythm and register feel larger.

From a loop to a pre chorus

Increase dominant function, rhythmic subdivision, or repetition. End on V, V7, or a suspended chord so the chorus feels necessary.

From a loop to a bridge

Use a relative key, borrowed chord, or modal color. Reduce the hook at first, then bring it back as recognition.

The Four Reliable Strategies

  1. Rotate the progression. Start the same chords from a different point. I V vi IV can become vi IV I V for a more minor opening.
  2. Borrow one chord. Replace one diatonic chord with a borrowed color such as bVII, iv, bVI, or bIII.
  3. Answer the melody. Keep the rhythm of the hook, but move the contour in the opposite direction.
  4. Change density. Verse is fewer notes. Chorus is more register and support. Build is repetition plus rising tension.

A Practical Session Workflow

Duplicate the clip. Never overwrite the first idea.
Mute half the notes. Find the simplest version that still has the identity.
Create an answer phrase. Use the same rhythm, but change the last note or last chord.
Make three versions. One smaller, one bigger, one weirder.
Arrange A B A. If returning to A feels good after B, the new section works.

Quick Diagnosis

If the new part sounds unrelated, you changed too many dimensions. Keep the rhythm or bass shape. If it sounds boring, you changed too few dimensions. Change register, density, or harmonic function. If it sounds wrong but interesting, check whether the wrong note is a passing tone, extension, borrowed color, or true clash.

What Theory Aide Can Help You Ask

Use Harmonic Timeline to compare the current idea and the next section. Look for:

  • Does the key stay the same, or does the new section imply a related center?
  • Does the tension strip rise into a chorus or drop into a verse?
  • Are the same modal colors showing up, or did the new section introduce a new color?
  • Does the section end unresolved, and does the suggested next chord point back to your main idea?
