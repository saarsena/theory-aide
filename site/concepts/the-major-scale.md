Look at the gaps between the notes on the grid, not the notes themselves.
The climb goes: two rows, two rows, one row, two, two, two, one. That
sequence of [[semitone]] steps, 2 2 1 2 2 2 1, is the entire definition of
the major [[scale]]. Everything else about it follows from those seven
numbers.

## The uneven ladder

The pattern's power is that it is *uneven*. If the steps were all the same
size, every rung would feel identical and you could never tell where you
were. The two half-step rungs break the symmetry, and the break gives every
position its own personality: the settled floor of the first note, the
bright lift of the third, the almost-home lean of the seventh. Do re mi is
not a children's song; it is your ear naming positions on an uneven ladder,
and it works because the unevenness makes each position recognizable.

## One pattern, twelve homes

The slider demonstrated the deepest property: the scale is portable.
Because it is defined by [[intervals|distances]], not notes, starting the
pattern from G or E♭ builds a scale that works exactly like the one from C,
just at a different altitude. This is the interval article's transposition
trick at full scale, and it is why "the major scale" is singular: there is
one pattern, wearing twelve addresses.

## In your music

Live has this ladder built in: set a scale in the clip view and the piano
roll can highlight or fold to exactly these rows, so drawing in key stops
being a memory exercise. The Theory Aide extension reads the same setting
as its frame of reference for everything it explains. And the obvious next
question, why picking a ladder makes one note feel like *home*, is exactly
where this trail goes next.
